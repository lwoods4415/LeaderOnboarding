## Module 0: The Five Myths New Managers Believe
*About 20 minutes*

> **The big idea:** Most new leaders struggle not because they lack skill, but because they start with the wrong picture of what the job is.

### Why this matters now
Before you lead anyone, you already carry a picture of what leading looks like. Usually it comes from bosses you've had, good and bad. That picture shapes your first conversations, your first decisions, and how your team decides to see you.

Harvard professor Linda Hill followed new managers through their first year and found that most of them started with the same five beliefs. Each one sounds reasonable. Each one quietly causes problems. Spotting them now, before day one, saves you months of learning them the hard way.

### The model: Myth vs. reality
*Five beliefs most new managers start with, and what the job actually asks.*

**How it works on screen:** Read each myth and decide whether you believe it, at least a little. Then flip the card to see what Hill's research found.

- **Myth 1: Managers have a lot of authority.**
  - Reality: New managers are surprised by how dependent they become. Results now depend on your team, your peers and your own leader. You have less control over outcomes than you did in your old role, not more.
  - *Example:* As a nurse, your call completion depended on you. As a lead, it depends on seven people, a schedule, a system and a patient population you don't control.
- **Myth 2: Authority comes with the title.**
  - Reality: The title gives you a job. Your team decides whether to give you authority, based on three things: your character (do you mean well and do what's right?), your competence (do you know the work?), and your follow-through (do things actually happen when you say they will?).
  - *Example:* A nurse doesn't follow your new workflow because you're the lead. She follows it because she trusts you understand her day and that you'll fix what isn't working.
- **Myth 3: Managers need to control their team.**
  - Reality: Control gets you compliance, and compliance lasts only as long as you're watching. What you actually need is commitment: people who do the right thing because they understand why and helped shape how.
  - *Example:* Checking every chart gets charts done this week. Explaining why documentation matters for patients, and asking how to make it easier, gets charts done every week.
- **Myth 4: Leading means building great one-on-one relationships.**
  - Reality: Individual relationships matter, but they aren't enough. If you only lead person by person, the team can splinter, and people start comparing how they're treated. You also have to lead the team as a whole: shared norms, fair treatment and a culture everyone is part of.
  - *Example:* A lead who is close with two nurses and distant with the rest will hear about “favorites,” even if she's never treated anyone unfairly.
- **Myth 5: My job is to keep things running smoothly.**
  - Reality: Keeping things running is only half the job. The other half is making things better: noticing what's broken, raising it, and improving how the team works, even when that's messier than leaving it alone.
  - *Example:* A smooth month with the same workaround everyone complains about isn't success. Fixing the workaround is.

### Why it works
Hill followed 19 new managers through their first year. Almost all of them described the transition as more painful and confusing than they expected. One said, “I never knew a promotion could be so painful.” Most of that pain came from the gap between what they thought the job was and what it really was. They learned by trial and error, often without realizing they were learning. The good news in her research: struggling early is normal, and the vast majority of new managers come through it.

The stakes are real. Gallup estimates that managers account for at least 70% of the difference in employee engagement between teams. In other words, how you lead shapes how your team feels about their work more than almost anything else.

Google's long-running study of its own managers, Project Oxygen, found that the top behavior of its best managers was being a good coach. It wasn't technical expertise or control. That's the shift this program is built around: from being great at the work to helping others be great at it.

*Sources:* [Linda A. Hill, “Becoming the Boss,” Harvard Business Review, January 2007](https://hbr.org/2007/01/becoming-the-boss); [Gallup, “Managers Account for 70% of Variance in Employee Engagement”](https://news.gallup.com/businessjournal/182792/managers-account-variance-employee-engagement.aspx); [Google re:Work, “Following the data: The research behind great managers”](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/following-the-data-the-research-behind-great-managers)

### See it in action
It's your first team huddle as the new lead. Until last week, you were a nurse on this same team. Here are two ways to open.

**Leading from the myths**
- *Leader:* Hi everyone. So, as you know, I'm the lead now.  
  ↳ Myth 2: leaning on the title. It also puts distance between you and the team on day one.
- *Leader:* Things are going to tighten up. I'll be checking everyone's numbers every day.  
  ↳ Myth 3: control. The team hears “I don't trust you,” and nobody has done anything wrong yet.
- *Leader:* I want everyone documenting the way I always did. It just works better.  
  ↳ Myth 1: assuming authority to change how people work before you understand why they work that way.
- *Team member:* (Silence. A few cameras go off.)
- **Result:** The team complies on the surface and starts talking about you in side chats. You've made your first weeks harder.

**Leading from reality**
- *Leader:* I know this is a change for all of us, me included. I'm still the same person, and my job is different now.  
  ↳ Honest about the change, without pretending nothing is different.
- *Leader:* Over the next two weeks I'll set up a one-on-one with each of you. I want to hear what's working, what's frustrating, and what you need from me.  
  ↳ Building credibility through listening and follow-through, not the title.
- *Leader:* I'm not planning to change how you work right away. I want to understand it first.  
  ↳ Commitment before control.
- *Leader:* One thing I do want us to work on together is making coverage fair for everyone. I'll bring ideas, and I want yours too.  
  ↳ Leading the team as a whole, and naming one thing to make better.
- **Result:** The team relaxes. People start booking their one-on-ones. You've begun earning authority instead of announcing it.

**Takeaway:** In the first weeks, your team is deciding who you are as a leader. Listening, following through and treating everyone the same earns you more authority than any announcement.

### Common traps
- **Proving yourself by working harder at your old job.** Your old job is no longer how you'll be measured. Put the extra hours into your people, not into being the best nurse on the team.
- **Swinging to one extreme: the strict boss or the best friend.** Aim for warm and clear. You can care about people and still hold a standard.
- **Changing a lot in your first month to show you're in charge.** Learn first. Change one or two things that the team agrees need fixing.
- **Treating early confusion as proof you're not cut out for this.** Feeling lost early is the normal experience. Talk about it with your own leader instead of hiding it.

### Check your understanding
1. A new lead announced a documentation change two weeks ago. Most of the team still isn't following it. What's the most likely problem?
    - ○ She doesn't have enough authority yet. *More authority would get, at best, compliance while she's watching. That's myth 1 and myth 3.*
    - ✅ She announced the change instead of explaining why and involving the team. *Right. Commitment comes from understanding why and having a say in how. Announcements get compliance at best.*
    - ○ She needs to check charts more often. *Monitoring might bump the numbers briefly, but it teaches the team that the change only matters when she's looking.*
2. Which is the best sign that a new leader is earning real authority?
    - ○ The team follows the rules when the leader is watching. *That's compliance, which disappears when you look away.*
    - ✅ Team members bring problems to the leader early, before they grow. *Yes. People bring problems early to leaders they trust and respect. That's authority you've earned.*
    - ○ Everyone on the team likes the leader. *Being liked is nice but isn't the goal. Teams can like a leader they don't trust to handle hard things.*
3. A lead has strong one-on-one relationships with each nurse, but the team keeps splitting into groups who only cover for each other. Which myth is she most likely stuck in?
    - ✅ Myth 4: leading means building great one-on-one relationships. *Right. She's leading person by person but not leading the team as a whole: shared norms, fair coverage, one culture.*
    - ○ Myth 5: my job is to keep things running smoothly. *Close, but the core issue is that she isn't building the team as a team.*
    - ○ Myth 2: authority comes with the title. *Her relationships suggest she's earning trust individually. The gap is at the team level.*

## Module 1: The Trust Equation and the Marble Jar
*About 25 minutes*

> **The big idea:** Trust isn't a feeling you wait for. It's built from four things you can see and change, and the one that matters most is how much you make it about yourself.

### Why this matters now
Every hard thing you'll do as a leader runs on trust: giving feedback, asking for change, holding a standard. Feedback from someone you trust sounds like help. The same words from someone you don't trust sound like criticism.

Trust can feel vague, as if people either click with you or they don't. This lesson breaks it into four parts you can actually work on, starting this week.

### The model: The trust equation
*Trust grows with credibility, reliability and closeness, and shrinks with self-focus.*

**How it works on screen:** Pick one person on your team. Move each slider to where you honestly think they'd rate you today, from 1 (low) to 10 (high). Watch what happens to the result, especially when you change self-focus.

**Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Closeness) ÷ Self-focus**

- **Credibility** (above the line): Can I believe what you say?
  - Low sounds like: “She says she'll look into it, but I'm not sure she actually knows how the system works.”
  - High sounds like: “If she says that's the right workflow, it is. And if she doesn't know, she says so.”
  - Build it: Say “I don't know, but I'll find out” instead of guessing. · Get the facts right before you share an update. · Know the work well enough to give useful help.
- **Reliability** (above the line): Do you do what you say you'll do?
  - Low sounds like: “She said she'd fix the coverage schedule weeks ago.”
  - High sounds like: “If she says she'll get back to me Thursday, she does.”
  - Build it: Make small promises and keep them, within 48 hours when you can. · If you can't keep a promise, say so before the deadline, not after. · Show up on time and on camera, consistently.
- **Closeness** (above the line): Do I feel safe telling you the real thing?
  - Low sounds like: “I'd never tell her I'm struggling. It would end up in my review.”
  - High sounds like: “I told her I was overwhelmed, and she helped me sort it out.”
  - Build it: Keep confidences. What's shared with you stays with you. · Respond to hard news with curiosity, not judgment. · Learn what matters to each person outside their metrics.
- **Self-focus** (below the line): Is this about you, or about me?
  - Low sounds like: “She's genuinely trying to help me get better.”
  - High sounds like: “Everything she does is about making herself look good to leadership.”
  - Build it: Listen more than you talk in one-on-ones. · Resist turning their story into your story (“When I was on the floor…”). · Ask “What would help you?” before sharing what you need.

Result bands: Fragile (Trust is thin right now. Start with one small promise you can keep this week, and listen more than you talk in your next one-on-one.) / Building (There's a foundation. Notice how much the score moves when self-focus goes down. That's usually the fastest lever for a new leader.) / Solid (This relationship can handle honest feedback. Protect it by staying reliable, especially when you're busy.) / Strong (This person likely brings you problems early. Ask yourself what you did to build this, and do it with the rest of the team.)

### Why it works
The trust equation comes from the book The Trusted Advisor (2000) by David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford. They noticed that people trust advisors for a few distinct reasons, and that one thing, self-orientation, can cancel out all the others. Because it sits underneath the line, it divides everything above it. You can be credible, reliable and warm, and if people sense it's really about you, trust drops sharply.

This is the trap new leaders fall into most. Early in a new role, it's natural to worry about how you're doing, whether you look capable, and what your own leader thinks. Your team can feel that. The fastest way to build trust is often not doing more, but making the conversation less about yourself.

The trust equation tells you what trust is made of. Brené Brown's research, in the next section, tells you how it actually gets built: slowly, and in moments so small they're easy to miss. Her BRAVING model (boundaries, reliability, accountability, vault, integrity, non-judgment, generosity) breaks trust into seven behaviors. You can rate yourself on it in the Toolkit's trust self-check.

*Sources:* [Trusted Advisor Associates, “The Trust Equation”](https://trustedadvisor.com/build-trust/trust-equation); David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford, The Trusted Advisor (2000)

### Spotlight: The marble jar
If the trust equation explains what trust is made of, Brené Brown's marble jar explains how it's built. It's one of the most useful pictures of trust a leader can carry.

The idea started with Brown's daughter, Ellen, in third grade. At recess, Ellen told a couple of friends something embarrassing that had happened to her. By the time class started, everyone knew, and kids were laughing and pointing. Ellen's teacher used a marble jar in class: marbles went in when students made good choices together, and came out when they didn't. That day, marbles came out. Ellen came home and said she would never trust anyone again.

Brown told her that trust works like a marble jar. You don't share your hardest things with just anyone. You share them with people who've filled up your jar over time. When she asked Ellen who her “marble jar friends” were, the answers were small: a friend who scooted over to make room at lunch, and another who noticed Ellen's grandparents at her soccer game and remembered their names.

Brown's research found the same thing in adults: “Trust is built in very small moments.” People described trusting someone because they asked how a family member's chemotherapy was going, because they showed up at a funeral, or because they asked for help when they needed it. Nobody described a grand gesture.

Marbles also come out in small moments. Brown draws on marriage researcher John Gottman's idea of “sliding door moments”: everyday chances to connect or turn away. In Gottman's words, “to choose to not connect when the opportunity is there, is a betrayal.” Brown also warns about gossip. Sharing something that isn't yours to share can feel like closeness in the moment, but she calls closeness built on talking about other people “counterfeit trust.” The person you're venting to quietly learns that you'd talk about them too.

- **Small beats big.** You can't fill a jar with one big gesture, like a team lunch or a speech about your open door. Marbles come from hundreds of small, consistent moments: remembering, following up, showing up.
- **Every person has their own jar.** You don't have one jar with your team. You have a separate jar with each person. Your jar with the person you click with may be full while your jar with the quietest person on your team is nearly empty.
- **Hard conversations draw on the jar.** Every piece of honest feedback, every “I need you to change this,” takes marbles out. If the jar is empty, the conversation feels like an attack. If it's full, the same words feel like help. Fill the jar before you need it.
- **Your sliding door moments are constant.** As a leader, people approach you all day: a quick message, a question at the end of a meeting, a sigh on camera. Each one is a chance to put a marble in or let one roll away.

**Activity: Marble in or marble out?.** Tap each moment, then choose whether it puts a marble in the jar or takes marbles out.

- Remembering that a nurse's son had a recital, and asking how it went → **Marble in**. Remembering what matters to someone is exactly the kind of small moment Brown's research describes.
- Getting back to someone on Thursday, like you said you would → **Marble in**. Reliability, one kept promise at a time.
- Telling one nurse about another nurse's personal struggles “so she'll understand” → **Marbles out**. That story wasn't yours to share. Both nurses learn that you don't keep confidences.
- Reading messages while a nurse tells you something hard → **Marbles out**. A sliding door moment where you turned away. She'll think twice before telling you something hard again.
- Asking your team for help with something you don't know how to do → **Marble in**. People in Brown's research trusted others who asked for help when they needed it. It shows honesty about your limits.
- Venting to one nurse about another nurse's attitude → **Marbles out**. Counterfeit trust. It feels like bonding, but the nurse you're venting to learns you'd talk about her too.
- Being on camera, on time, for every huddle → **Marble in**. Showing up consistently is a marble your whole team sees, every day.
- Saying “Let's talk later” when a nurse stops you, then never coming back to it → **Marbles out**. A small broken promise. Following up later would have turned it into a marble in.
- Admitting a mistake you made, and fixing it → **Marble in**. Accountability is one of the seven parts of BRAVING. Owning a miss builds trust faster than hiding it.

*Sources:* [Brené Brown, “Brené on the Anatomy of Trust,” Unlocking Us podcast (with full transcript)](https://brenebrown.com/books/the-anatomy-of-trust/); Brené Brown, Dare to Lead; John Gottman, The Science of Trust

### See it in action
It's your first real one-on-one with Marcus, a quiet nurse who joined six months ago. Here are two versions of the first few minutes.

**High self-focus**
- *Leader:* So, I wanted to tell you about my plans for the pod now that I'm lead.  
  ↳ Self-focus. The meeting starts with your agenda, not his.
- *Leader:* When I was a nurse here, I always did my calls first thing. You should try it.  
  ↳ Turning his work into your story. It also lowers closeness, because he hears judgment.
- *Team member:* Okay, sure.
- *Leader:* And don't worry, I'll get your schedule fixed by next week.  
  ↳ A big promise you may not be able to keep. Breaking it costs reliability.
- **Result:** Marcus leaves polite and closed. He won't bring you a problem until it's serious.

**Low self-focus**
- *Leader:* This time is for you. What's been on your mind lately?  
  ↳ Low self-focus from the first sentence.
- *Team member:* Honestly, it's been a lot. I'm still figuring out the remote setup.
- *Leader:* Tell me more. What's been the hardest part?  
  ↳ Following up shows you're actually listening. That builds closeness.
- *Leader:* I don't know the answer on the schedule yet. I'll find out and get back to you by Thursday.  
  ↳ Honest about what you don't know (credibility), and a small promise you can keep (reliability).
- **Result:** Marcus opens up a little. When Thursday comes and you follow through, he opens up more.

**Takeaway:** You can't ask for trust. You can only make it easy to give: keep your word, be honest about what you don't know, and make the conversation about them.

### Common traps
- **Mistaking being liked for being trusted.** A team can like you and still not believe you'll handle hard things. Trust shows when people bring you bad news.
- **Making big promises to win people over.** Make small promises and keep every one. Reliability is built in small, repeated moments.
- **Oversharing to seem relatable.** Closeness means they feel safe with you, not that you share your personal problems with them. Your team shouldn't have to carry your stress.
- **Building trust only with the people you click with.** Be deliberate about the people you connect with least. They're watching how you treat everyone.

## Module 2: Lead Measures and Lag Measures
*About 20 minutes*

> **The big idea:** Month-end numbers tell you whether your team won. Lead measures tell you, this week, whether you're going to.

### Why this matters now
As a nurse, your scoreboard was clear and fast. You knew by the end of each day whether you were on track. As a leader, your results arrive weeks later, through other people's work. By the time a month-end report shows a problem, the month is over.

That gap is why so many new leaders feel less capable and drift back to doing the work themselves. This lesson gives you a new scoreboard: a few things you control every week that predict your team's results.

### The model: Lead or lag?
*Lag measures report the result. Lead measures predict it and are in your control this week.*

**How it works on screen:** Sort each measure. Ask two questions: “Will I only know this after it's too late to change?” (lag) and “Can I act on this every week, and does it predict the result?” (lead).

**Lag measure:** The result. You find out after the fact, when it can't be changed.  
**Lead measure:** An action you control this week that predicts the result.

- The pod's monitoring-call completion at month end → **Lag measure**. This is the result you care about, but by the time you see it the month is over.
- Number of one-on-ones you held this week → **Lead measure**. You control it every week, and regular one-on-ones predict problems reaching you early.
- Patients with controlled blood pressure at quarter end → **Lag measure**. An important outcome, but it reports what already happened.
- Days between noticing a missed standard and talking about it → **Lead measure**. Fully in your control, and prompt conversations predict fewer repeat problems.
- Your team's engagement survey score → **Lag measure**. It reflects months of experience. You can't change it the week it arrives.
- Mid-month pace check-ins with nurses who are behind → **Lead measure**. You can do this every week, and it predicts who will hit the month-end target.
- Turnover on your team this year → **Lag measure**. A long-term result of how the team has been led.
- Times this week you named a specific thing someone did well → **Lead measure**. In your control, and specific recognition predicts engagement and repeated good work.
- Client complaints this quarter → **Lag measure**. You only learn about these after something has gone wrong.

### Why it works
This idea comes from The 4 Disciplines of Execution (4DX) by Chris McChesney, Sean Covey and Jim Huling. Their research found that teams often fail at important goals not because the goal is wrong, but because daily urgent work, which they call the whirlwind, swallows the time. A lag measure tells you whether you reached the goal. A good lead measure has two qualities: it predicts the lag measure, and the team can influence it directly.

The four disciplines build on each other. Focus on one or two wildly important goals. Act on the lead measures. Keep a scoreboard people can see at a glance. Then create a cadence of accountability: a short, regular check-in where people report on last week's commitments and make new ones.

For a new leader, lead measures solve a second problem. They replace the daily proof you used to get from your own numbers. Each week you can see that you did the things that make your team succeed, even before the results show up.

*Sources:* [FranklinCovey, “Discipline 2: Act on the Lead Measures”](https://www.franklincovey.com/the-4-disciplines/discipline-2-act/); Chris McChesney, Sean Covey and Jim Huling, The 4 Disciplines of Execution (2012; revised 2021)

### See it in action
It's the middle of the month. Your own leader asks, “How's the pod tracking this month?” Here are two answers.

**Lag-only thinking**
- *Leader:* I think we're okay. Last month we finished at 93%.  
  ↳ Last month's lag measure tells you nothing about this month.
- *Leader:* I'll know more when the month-end report comes out.  
  ↳ By then it's too late to help anyone who's behind.
- *Leader:* I've been really busy covering alerts, so I haven't had a chance to look closely.  
  ↳ The whirlwind won. The urgent work pushed out the work that predicts results.
- **Result:** At month end, two nurses miss target. You learn about it at the same time as your leader.

**Lead-measure thinking**
- *Leader:* We're at 51% of calls with half the month gone, so slightly ahead of pace overall.  
  ↳ Tracking the lag measure as it builds, not only at the end.
- *Leader:* Two nurses are behind pace. I checked in with both on Tuesday.  
  ↳ A lead measure: mid-month pace check-ins.
- *Leader:* One has a panel issue we're fixing. The other is catching up and we're checking in again Friday.  
  ↳ Acting on it while there's still time.
- *Leader:* I also held all six one-on-ones this week.  
  ↳ Another lead measure that predicts problems reaching you early.
- **Result:** Both nurses finish the month on target. You saw the problem with two weeks to spare.

**Takeaway:** You can't manage a lag measure directly. You manage the lead measures that drive it, every week, and the lag measure follows.

### Common traps
- **Tracking ten things.** Pick two or three lead measures. Too many measures means none of them get attention.
- **Choosing lead measures you don't actually control.** If you can't act on it this week, it's not a lead measure for you.
- **Using the scoreboard to watch people.** A scoreboard is for the team to see if it's winning, not for you to catch people out.
- **Letting the whirlwind skip your weekly check.** Put your lead-measure review on the calendar at the same time each week and protect it.

### Check your understanding
1. The goal is for every nurse to reach 95% monitoring-call completion. Which of these is a lead measure?
    - ○ Each nurse's completion percentage at month end *That's the lag measure: the result itself.*
    - ✅ Weekly pace check-ins with any nurse who's falling behind *Right. You control it weekly, and it predicts who will reach 95%.*
    - ○ The number of nurses at 95% last month *That's a past lag measure. It can't be changed.*
2. What two qualities make a good lead measure?
    - ○ It's easy to count and it looks good in reports. *Easy to count helps, but a lead measure has to drive the result and be in your control.*
    - ✅ It predicts the result, and your team can influence it directly. *Yes. Predictive and influenceable are the two tests.*
    - ○ Leadership chose it, and it's reported monthly. *Measures reported monthly are usually lag measures.*
3. A lead found out from the month-end report that her pod dropped 8 points. What would have helped most?
    - ○ A more detailed month-end report *More detail on a lag measure still arrives too late.*
    - ✅ Tracking a few lead measures every week, with a regular check-in *Right. That would have shown the drop while there was still time to act.*
    - ○ Covering more alerts herself *That's the whirlwind. It usually makes the problem worse.*

## Module 3: The GROW Coaching Model
*About 20 minutes*

> **The big idea:** Every time you solve a problem someone could have solved, you teach them to bring you the next one. Coaching is a simple structure for helping them solve it instead.

### Why this matters now
You were promoted partly because you're good at fixing things. That instinct now works against you. If you answer every question, your team stops thinking and starts waiting, and you become the bottleneck.

Coaching isn't a personality trait or a long, formal process. It's a short conversation with four steps that you can use in a five-minute message exchange. This lesson teaches the most widely used one.

### The model: GROW
*Goal, Reality, Options, Will: four steps that move someone from stuck to a plan they own.*

**How it works on screen:** Work through the four steps in order. Each one shows what it's for, what it sounds like, and the mistake to watch for. The example follows one real situation from start to finish.

**G: Goal: Get clear on what they want from this conversation.**

Start by agreeing what you're trying to solve. People often bring you a symptom. A clear goal keeps you both from solving the wrong problem.

*Example:* Priya: “My patient won't take daily readings. What do I do?” You: “What would a good outcome look like with this patient?” Priya: “Honestly, just getting her to take them most days.”
*Questions to ask:* What would you like to get out of this? · What would a good outcome look like? · What's the real challenge here for you?
*Watch for:* Jumping to solutions before you both know what problem you're solving.

**R: Reality: Understand what's actually happening.**

Ask about the facts, what they've already tried, and what they've noticed. This is usually where people discover they know more than they thought.

*Example:* You: “What's happening right now, and what have you tried?” Priya: “I called twice. She says she forgets. Actually, she also said the cuff is confusing.”
*Questions to ask:* What's happening right now? · What have you already tried? · What do you think is getting in the way? · And what else?
*Watch for:* Accepting the first answer. “And what else?” almost always surfaces the real issue.

**O: Options: Generate possible ways forward, theirs first.**

Ask for their ideas before offering yours. If there's a real knowledge gap, this is where you teach, briefly, then hand it back.

*Example:* You: “What could you try?” Priya: “Maybe walk her through the device on video?” You: “Good idea. One thing that also helps: tying the reading to something she already does daily, like morning coffee.”
*Questions to ask:* What could you try? · What else might work? · Who or what could help? · If you had no limits, what would you do?
*Watch for:* Advice disguised as a question, like “Have you thought about calling her daughter?” Ask openly first.

**W: Will: Agree on what they'll actually do, and when.**

End with a commitment in their words: the next step, the timing, and how they'll know it worked. Then agree when you'll check in.

*Example:* You: “So what will you do first?” Priya: “Video call tomorrow to walk through the cuff, then link it to her morning routine.” You: “Great. Let me know Friday how it went.”
*Questions to ask:* What will you do first, and when? · How confident are you, from 1 to 10? · What support do you need from me?
*Watch for:* Ending on “Let me know if you need anything.” Without a clear next step, nothing changes.

> Coach when the goal is growth. Tell when someone could be harmed, when time is critical, or when the person genuinely doesn't have the knowledge yet, and then debrief afterward so they learn for next time.

### Why it works
GROW was developed in the 1980s by coaches including Sir John Whitmore, and became widely known through his book Coaching for Performance. It works because it follows how people actually solve problems: get clear on the goal, look honestly at the situation, consider options, then commit. When someone reaches a plan through their own thinking, they understand it better and are more likely to follow through.

Google's long-running study of its own managers, Project Oxygen, found that the number one behavior of its best managers was being a good coach. Michael Bungay Stanier, author of The Coaching Habit, describes the urge to jump in with answers as your “advice monster.” His simplest tool is the question “And what else?”, because the first answer someone gives is rarely the whole story.

Liz Wiseman's research on “multipliers” found that leaders who rescue too quickly shrink their team's capability without meaning to. People stop building their own judgment because someone else keeps doing it for them. Coaching feels slower today and saves you hours every week once your team can solve problems on their own.

*Sources:* [Performance Consultants, “The GROW Model: A Complete Guide”](https://www.performanceconsultants.com/resources/the-grow-model/); [Google re:Work, “Following the data: The research behind great managers”](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/following-the-data-the-research-behind-great-managers); [Michael Bungay Stanier, “How to tame your advice monster,” TEDxUniversityofNevada](https://www.ted.com/talks/michael_bungay_stanier_how_to_tame_your_advice_monster); John Whitmore, Coaching for Performance; Liz Wiseman, Multipliers

### See it in action
Priya, a newer nurse, messages you: “Patient keeps refusing daily readings. What should I do?” The patient isn't in any danger. Here are two ways to respond.

**Rescuing**
- *Leader:* Send me her name and I'll call her this afternoon.  
  ↳ The classic rescue. The patient gets a call, and Priya learns hard calls go up the chain.
- *Team member:* Thank you!! I have two more like that, could you take those too?
- *Leader:* Sure, send them over.  
  ↳ Your workload just grew, and so will next week's.
- **Result:** Three patients get calls. Priya doesn't learn anything, and she'll bring you the next one.

**Coaching with GROW**
- *Leader:* What would a good outcome look like with her?  
  ↳ Goal: clarify what you're solving.
- *Leader:* What have you tried so far? And what else have you noticed?  
  ↳ Reality, with “And what else?”
- *Team member:* I called twice. Oh, actually, she said the cuff confuses her.
- *Leader:* That's a great catch. What could you try?  
  ↳ Options: hers first. She's now solving the real problem.
- *Team member:* Maybe walk her through it on video tomorrow?
- *Leader:* Love it. Let me know Friday how it goes.  
  ↳ Will: a clear next step and a check-in.
- **Result:** Priya solves it herself, and next time she may not need to message you at all.

**Takeaway:** The goal of coaching isn't to withhold answers. It's to help someone find the answer they already half-know, and teach only what they're missing.

### Common traps
- **Thinking coaching means never giving an answer.** Ask first. If there's a real knowledge gap, teach it briefly, then hand the decision back.
- **Asking advice disguised as a question.** “Have you tried calling her daughter?” is advice. Ask “What could you try?” first.
- **Skipping the W.** Always end with a specific next step and a check-in time. Otherwise it's just a nice chat.
- **Coaching in an emergency.** If a patient is at risk, act first. Debrief afterward so the nurse learns for next time.

### Check your understanding
1. A nurse asks what to do about a patient who hasn't sent readings in five days. The patient isn't at risk. What's the best first response?
    - ○ Tell her the steps. It's faster. *Faster today, but she'll ask again next time, and so will others.*
    - ✅ Ask what she's tried so far and what she thinks the next step is. *Right. Start with Reality and Options, then confirm or add to her plan.*
    - ○ Call the patient yourself. *That's a rescue. She loses the chance to build the skill.*
2. Which question belongs in the Reality step?
    - ○ What would success look like? *That's a Goal question.*
    - ✅ What's happening right now, and what have you tried? *Yes. Reality is about the facts and what's already been done.*
    - ○ What will you do first? *That's a Will question.*
3. When should a leader tell instead of coach?
    - ○ Whenever it's faster. *Telling is almost always faster in the moment. That's why rescuing is so tempting.*
    - ✅ When a patient is at risk, time is critical, or the person truly lacks the knowledge. *Right. In those cases, tell, and debrief afterward so they learn.*
    - ○ Never. Good leaders always coach. *Coaching in an emergency can put patients at risk.*

### Make it yours
Think of the last problem someone brought you that you solved for them. Rewrite it as a coaching conversation. Your answers are private unless you choose to share them.

1. What was the problem, and what did you do?
    - ✍️ _Answer box: “They asked me… and I…”_
2. Goal: what question could you have asked to clarify what they wanted?
    - ✍️ _Answer box: “I could have asked…”_
3. Reality: what would you ask to understand what was really happening?
    - ✍️ _Answer box: “I could have asked…”_
4. Options: how would you have drawn out their ideas first?
    - ✍️ _Answer box: “I could have asked…”_

    - ✍️ _Answer box: “I could have closed with…”_

- Reality: What's happening? What have you tried? And what else?

## Module 4: The Path to Action
*About 20 minutes*

> **The big idea:** Between what someone does and how you react, you tell yourself a story. The story drives your feelings and your actions, and most of the time you don't notice you told it.

### Why this matters now
As a peer, you could think “she just doesn't care” about a coworker, and it cost nothing. As a leader, that same thought shapes how you talk to her, what you write down, and what happens to her job. Your stories now have consequences.

This lesson gives you a simple map of how stories form, and how to get back to the facts before a hard conversation, so you open with what happened instead of what you assumed.

### The model: The Path to Action
*See and hear → Tell a story → Feel → Act. We usually skip from the first step straight to the last.*

**How it works on screen:** Click through the four steps. Each one follows the same situation, so you can see where the story slips in and how to catch it.

**1. See and hear: The facts: what a camera would have recorded.**

Facts are things anyone could observe: numbers, dates, words actually said, actions actually taken. They're the least controversial part of any conversation, which makes them the safest place to start.

*Example:* Dana's monitoring-call completion was 82% last month and 79% this month. The target is 95%.
*Questions to ask:* What did I actually see or hear? · Would someone else, watching, describe it the same way?
*Watch for:* Words like always, never, attitude, lazy or doesn't care. Those aren't facts, they're conclusions.

**2. Tell a story: The meaning you add to the facts, often in a split second.**

Your brain fills gaps automatically. It guesses why someone did something. That's normal and often useful, but the story is a guess, not a fact. The danger is forgetting that you made it up.

*Example:* “She just doesn't care anymore. She's checked out since the panel change.”
*Questions to ask:* What story am I telling myself? · Why would a reasonable, rational, decent person do this? · What am I pretending not to notice about my own part in this?
*Watch for:* Three common stories: the villain story (“it's her fault”), the victim story (“it's not my fault”), and the helpless story (“there's nothing I can do”).

**3. Feel: Your emotions come from the story, not from the facts.**

The same facts with a different story create a completely different feeling. If your story is “she doesn't care,” you'll feel frustrated. If your story is “something might be going on,” you'll feel concerned. Strong emotions are a signal to check your story.

*Example:* Frustrated, a little betrayed, maybe defensive about your pod's numbers.
*Questions to ask:* What am I feeling right now? · Which part of my story is creating this feeling?
*Watch for:* Treating your feeling as proof the story is true. Feeling strongly about something doesn't make it accurate.

**4. Act: What you do next, driven by the story you believe.**

If you act on an unchecked story, the other person reacts to your story, not to the facts. They defend themselves, and the real issue never gets solved. Going back along the path, from action to feeling to story to facts, lets you choose a better action.

*Example:* Unchecked: a cold email restating the target. Checked: a conversation that opens with the numbers and asks what's getting in the way.
*Questions to ask:* If I act on this story, what will happen? · What would I do if I only had the facts?
*Watch for:* Acting while the feeling is still hot. If you notice a strong emotion, retrace the path before you act.

> When you're ready to talk, use STATE from Crucial Conversations: Share your facts. Tell your story, as a possibility. Ask for their view. Talk tentatively. Encourage them to disagree with you.

### Why it works
The Path to Action comes from Crucial Conversations by Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny, Ron McMillan and Al Switzler. Their key insight is that other people don't make us angry. We make ourselves angry with the stories we tell about them. That's good news, because it means we can change the feeling by changing the story.

A related idea, the “ladder of inference,” was developed by organizational psychologist Chris Argyris. It describes how we climb from facts to assumptions to conclusions to beliefs so quickly that we don't notice the steps. Both models make the same point: slow down and climb back to what actually happened.

Starting with facts also matters because of how people react to perceived judgment. David Rock's SCARF model describes how threats to status, certainty, autonomy, relatedness or fairness can trigger a defensive response in the brain that makes clear thinking harder. Opening with a judgment, like “you don't seem invested,” threatens status and fairness. Opening with a fact lowers the threat, so the other person can actually engage.

*Sources:* [Crucial Learning, “Crucial Conversations Skill Summary: Master My Stories”](https://cruciallearning.com/blog/crucial-conversations-skill-summary-master-my-stories/); Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny, Ron McMillan and Al Switzler, Crucial Conversations; [David Rock, “SCARF: a brain-based model for collaborating with and influencing others,” NeuroLeadership Journal, 2008](https://schoolguide.casel.org/uploads/sites/2/2018/12/SCARF-NeuroleadershipArticle.pdf)

### See it in action
Dana has been a solid nurse for four years. Her call completion dropped to 82%, then 79%. Here are two ways to open the conversation.

**Opening with the story**
- *Leader:* I've noticed you don't seem as invested lately.  
  ↳ “Invested” is your story, not a fact. She'll argue with the story instead of discussing the numbers.
- *Team member:* Not invested? I've been here four years. I work harder than anyone on this pod.
- *Leader:* Then why have your numbers dropped two months in a row?  
  ↳ A question that's really an accusation. Now it's a debate.
- *Team member:* Because the targets are unrealistic, honestly.
- **Result:** Dana gets defensive, and the real reason for the drop never comes up.

**Opening with the facts**
- *Leader:* I want to talk about your call completion. It was 82% last month and 79% this month, and the target is 95%.  
  ↳ Facts only. Nothing to argue with.
- *Leader:* That's not like you. You've been one of our most consistent nurses for years.  
  ↳ This sets up mutual respect, and it's also true.
- *Leader:* I don't know what's going on yet. What's been getting in the way?  
  ↳ Curious, not certain. Talking tentatively.
- *Team member:* Honestly, I got 15 new complex patients two months ago, and it's been a lot.
- **Result:** You find a real barrier you can help solve, which the first version would never have uncovered.

**Takeaway:** Facts are hard to argue with and easy to discuss. Stories invite a debate. Open with what happened, then get curious about why.

### Common traps
- **Hiding the story inside a question.** “Is everything okay with your motivation?” is still a story. Ask “What's been getting in the way?”
- **Calling a story a fact.** “You're always late” is a story. “Notes were submitted late on Sept 22, Oct 3 and Oct 9” is a fact.
- **Never sharing what you're concerned about.** After the facts, it's okay to share your concern as a possibility: “I'm starting to wonder if something's going on. Is that right?”
- **Having the conversation while you're still angry.** Retrace your path first. If you can't name the facts calmly, you're not ready yet.

### Check your understanding
1. Which of these is a fact?
    - ○ She's always late with her notes. *“Always” is a conclusion. It's your story about the facts.*
    - ✅ Her notes were submitted more than 24 hours after the call on Sept 22, Oct 3 and Oct 9. *Right. Specific, observable, and anyone could verify it.*
    - ○ She doesn't prioritize documentation. *That's a guess about her priorities, not something you observed.*
2. You're frustrated because you believe a nurse is ignoring your messages. According to the Path to Action, what should you do first?
    - ○ Message her and say you've noticed she's ignoring you. *That acts on the story. She'll respond to the accusation, not the facts.*
    - ✅ Go back to what you actually saw: how many messages, when, and what happened. *Yes. Retrace the path to the facts, then ask yourself why a reasonable person might do this.*
    - ○ Wait until you feel less frustrated and let it go. *Waiting can help you cool down, but letting it go means the issue never gets solved.*
3. Which opening best follows the STATE approach?
    - ○ “Why don't you care about your numbers anymore?” *This opens with a story and puts her on the defensive.*
    - ✅ “Your completion was 82%, then 79%. I'm wondering if something's getting in the way. What's going on from your side?” *Right. Facts first, the story offered as a possibility, then a genuine question.*
    - ○ “I feel like you're checked out, and it's affecting the team.” *Sharing your feeling is fine, but this skips the facts and states the story as truth.*

### Make it yours
Think of someone on your team you're frustrated with right now. Use initials, not full names. Your answers are private unless you choose to share them.

1. What are the facts? Write only what you actually saw or heard.
    - ✍️ _Answer box: “On [dates], I saw…”_

## Module 5: Radical Candor and the Pattern Conversation
*About 20 minutes*

> **The big idea:** Caring about someone and being honest with them aren't opposites. The kindest leaders do both at once, especially with the people they like most.

### Why this matters now
Holding a friend or former peer to the same standard as everyone else is one of the hardest parts of a first leadership role. It's tempting to soften it, joke about it, or let it slide. Meanwhile, the rest of the team is watching to see whether the standard is real.

This lesson gives you two tools: a simple way to see when you're being too soft or too harsh, and a way to know which conversation to have when the same problem keeps happening.

### The model: Radical Candor
*Care personally and challenge directly. Drop either one and you land in a quadrant that hurts people.*

**How it works on screen:** Tap each quadrant to see what it looks like and how to move toward Radical Candor. Then use the exercise below the grid to place yourself with one real person on your team.

Axes: Challenge directly (left to right) and Care personally (bottom to top).

- **Radical Candor** (the goal): You say the hard thing clearly, and the person can tell you're on their side. Feedback lands as help.
  - Sounds like: “I care about you and want you to do well here, which is why I need to be straight with you about the late notes.”
  - Move: This is the goal. Keep feedback specific, private and prompt.
- **Ruinous Empathy**: You care, so you soften or skip the feedback. It feels kind, but the person never gets the information they need, and the problem grows.
  - Sounds like: “Don't worry about it, just get them in when you can.”
  - Move: Add the challenge. Name the specific issue and the standard, kindly but clearly.
- **Obnoxious Aggression**: You say the hard thing, but without care. It may be accurate, and it feels like an attack.
  - Sounds like: “This is the third time. Honestly, it's unprofessional.”
  - Move: Add the care. Show you're on their side and ask what's going on before you judge.
- **Manipulative Insincerity**: Neither care nor challenge: vague hints, talking about the person instead of to them, or false praise to avoid a hard moment.
  - Sounds like: A reminder to the whole team at huddle, aimed at one person.
  - Move: Go to the person directly, privately, with both care and the specific issue.

**Place yourself.** Think of the person on your team you find hardest to give feedback to, often a friend or former peer. Use initials, not full names.
- Which quadrant are you in with them right now, and why?
  - ✍️ _Answer box: “With [initials], I'm in… because…”_
- What would moving one step toward Radical Candor look like with this person?
  - ✍️ _Answer box: “One step closer would be…”_

### Why it works
Radical Candor was developed by Kim Scott, a former manager at Google and Apple. Her core point is that the two things good feedback needs, caring personally and challenging directly, are usually treated as a trade-off. They aren't. People accept hard feedback from someone they know cares about them, and caring without honesty eventually lets them down. With people we like, the most tempting quadrant is ruinous empathy, because it feels kind in the moment.

The second tool comes from Crucial Accountability by the authors of Crucial Conversations. They call it CPR: Content, Pattern, Relationship. The first time something happens, you talk about that one issue (content). If it keeps happening, you talk about the pattern instead of the latest example, which otherwise turns into a debate about special circumstances. If the pattern continues and starts to damage trust, you talk about the relationship: what the repeated problem is doing to your ability to rely on each other.

Many leaders get stuck repeating the content conversation. Each one feels reasonable, but nothing changes, and resentment builds on both sides. Naming the pattern is more honest, and it gives the person a fair chance to change before anything becomes formal. That connects directly to the Performance pathway in your Resources.

*Sources:* [Kim Scott, “Radical Candor: The Surprising Secret to Being a Good Boss,” First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/radical-candor-the-surprising-secret-to-being-a-good-boss); [Joseph Grenny, “Using CPR,” Crucial Learning](https://cruciallearning.com/blog/using-cpr/); Kim Scott, Radical Candor; Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny and others, Crucial Accountability

### See it in action
Tasha is your closest friend on the team. Three weeks ago you talked with her about late documentation. Since then it's happened twice more. This is the pattern conversation.

**Ruinous empathy**
- *Leader:* Hey, no big deal, but a couple of notes came in late again.  
  ↳ “No big deal” tells her it doesn't matter. That's ruinous empathy.
- *Team member:* Ugh, I know. I'll catch up this weekend!
- *Leader:* Okay, perfect. Thanks!  
  ↳ Stuck at the content level again, with no pattern named and no next step.
- **Result:** Nothing changes. The rest of the team notices, and in a month the conversation is much harder.

**Radical candor, at the pattern level**
- *Leader:* This is a little awkward because we're close, and I'd rather name that than pretend it isn't there.  
  ↳ Caring personally, and honest about the friendship.
- *Leader:* We talked about late notes three weeks ago. Since then it's happened on Oct 3 and Oct 9. So this isn't about one note anymore. It's a pattern.  
  ↳ Moving from content to pattern, with specific facts.
- *Leader:* I hold everyone to the same standard, and I want to for you too. What's really going on?  
  ↳ Challenging directly, then genuinely asking.
- *Team member:* Honestly? I've been picking up extra shifts somewhere else and I'm exhausted.
- *Leader:* Thank you for telling me. Let's figure out a plan that works. I'll also send you a short summary of what we agree, so we're both clear.  
  ↳ Support plus transparency about next steps. No surprises.
- **Result:** You learn what's really happening, the standard holds, and the friendship survives the honesty.

**Takeaway:** Letting a friend slide feels kind, but it's unfair to them and to everyone else meeting the standard. Care enough to be honest.

### Common traps
- **Thinking candor means being blunt.** Candor without care is obnoxious aggression. Show you're on their side first.
- **Only giving praise to people you like.** Specific praise and specific criticism both show you're paying attention. Give both.
- **Addressing one person's issue in a team message.** That's manipulative insincerity. Talk to the person directly and privately.
- **Having the same content conversation over and over.** The second time, name the pattern. The third time, talk about what it's doing to trust.

### Check your understanding
1. A lead tells her close friend on the team, “Don't worry about it,” about repeated late documentation. Which quadrant is that?
    - ○ Radical Candor *It's caring, but there's no challenge, so it's not candor.*
    - ✅ Ruinous Empathy *Right. Care without challenge. It feels kind but lets the problem grow.*
    - ○ Manipulative Insincerity *Close, but she does care. She's just avoiding the challenge.*
2. You've already talked with a nurse once about missed follow-ups. It's happened twice more. Using CPR, which conversation should you have?
    - ○ Content: talk about the most recent missed follow-up. *That invites a debate about that one instance. You've already had the content conversation.*
    - ✅ Pattern: name that this has happened repeatedly since you last talked. *Yes. The repetition is now the real issue.*
    - ○ Relationship: tell her you can't trust her anymore. *That's for when a pattern continues and starts damaging trust. It's too early here.*
3. Which statement is the best example of Radical Candor?
    - ○ “Great job lately! Oh, and maybe watch the notes a bit.” *The challenge is buried and vague, so it probably won't land.*
    - ✅ “I want you to succeed here, so I need to be clear: notes have been late three times this month. What's getting in the way?” *Right. Clear care, a specific challenge, and genuine curiosity.*
    - ○ “Your notes are late again. Fix it.” *Direct, but with no care. That's obnoxious aggression.*

## Module 6: Psychological Safety and Accountability
*About 20 minutes*

> **The big idea:** The best teams aren't the ones that make the fewest mistakes. They're the ones where people speak up about mistakes early, and are still held to a high standard.

### Why this matters now
Over the last eleven weeks you've built trust, coached, and held people to a standard. This lesson ties it together. Safety without standards becomes a comfortable team that doesn't improve. Standards without safety become a team that hides problems from you.

In patient care, hidden problems are dangerous. The way you respond when someone brings you bad news decides whether you'll hear the next piece of bad news, or whether you'll find out from a report, a client or a patient.

### The model: The four zones
*High safety and high accountability together create a learning team. Either one alone falls short.*

**How it works on screen:** Tap each zone to see what it feels like on a team and how a leader moves the team toward the learning zone. Then place your own team.

Axes: Accountability and standards (left to right) and Psychological safety (bottom to top).

- **Learning zone** (the goal): People raise problems, ask for help and admit mistakes early, and the bar stays high. The team gets better every month.
  - Sounds like: “I think I missed an escalation yesterday. Can we look at what happened?”
  - Move: This is the goal. Keep thanking people for bad news, and keep the standard clear.
- **Comfort zone**: It's friendly and safe, but nobody pushes. Missed standards get a pass, and good people quietly get frustrated.
  - Sounds like: “It's fine, everyone's doing their best.”
  - Move: Raise the bar. Make expectations clear and follow up when they're missed.
- **Anxiety zone**: Standards are high, but speaking up feels risky. People work hard and hide problems, so mistakes surface late, when they're bigger.
  - Sounds like: “I'm not telling her. I'll just fix it and hope she doesn't notice.”
  - Move: Lower the fear. Thank people for raising problems, and respond to mistakes with curiosity before anything else.
- **Apathy zone**: Low safety and low standards. People go through the motions and protect themselves.
  - Sounds like: “Why bother? Nothing changes anyway.”
  - Move: Start with safety. Listen, act visibly on what you hear, then build standards together.

**Place yourself.** Now place your own team.
- Which zone is your team in today?
  - ✍️ _Answer box: “I think we're in the… zone.”_
- What's one piece of evidence, something someone said or didn't say, that tells you that?
  - ✍️ _Answer box: “I noticed…”_
- What's one move you'll make this month to get closer to the learning zone?
  - ✍️ _Answer box: “This month I'll…”_

### Why it works
Psychological safety is a term from Harvard professor Amy Edmondson. It means people believe they can speak up, ask questions, admit mistakes and raise concerns without being punished or humiliated. In a study of hospital nursing units, she found that the better-led units reported more medication errors, not fewer. They weren't making more mistakes. People felt safe enough to report them, which meant the mistakes could be caught and fixed.

Edmondson is clear that safety is not about being nice or lowering standards. Her four-zone model shows that safety has to be paired with accountability. A team with safety but no standards sits in the comfort zone. A team with standards but no safety sits in the anxiety zone, which is common in high-pressure healthcare settings. Only the combination creates a learning zone.

Just culture, widely used in healthcare and aviation, puts this into practice. The American Nurses Association describes it as three kinds of behavior, each with a different response. A simple human error, an unintended slip, calls for consoling the person and reviewing the process. An at-risk behavior, such as a shortcut whose risk wasn't recognized, calls for coaching. Only reckless behavior, consciously ignoring a serious risk, calls for remedial or punitive action. The response depends on the behavior, not on how bad the outcome was.

*Sources:* [Amy Edmondson, “Building a psychologically safe workplace,” TEDxHGSE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LhoLuui9gX8); [American Nurses Association, Just Culture position statement (2010)](https://anaprodsite1.nursingworld.org/globalassets/practiceandpolicy/health-and-safety/just_culture.pdf); [AHRQ Patient Safety Network, “Culture of Safety” primer](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/primer/culture-safety); Amy Edmondson, The Fearless Organization (2018)

### See it in action
Leah, a nurse on your team, tells you she forgot to escalate an abnormal blood pressure reading yesterday. She caught it this morning, and the patient is fine.

**Anxiety zone response**
- *Leader:* Leah, this is serious. That patient could have been harmed.  
  ↳ True, but leading with it teaches her that telling you is risky.
- *Team member:* I know. I'm so sorry. It won't happen again.
- *Leader:* I'll need to document this.  
  ↳ The process may need documenting, but with no curiosity about why it happened, the cause stays hidden.
- **Result:** Leah never makes that mistake again, and she also never tells you about the next one.

**Learning zone response**
- *Leader:* Thank you for telling me. I know that wasn't easy.  
  ↳ Safety first. You're rewarding the report.
- *Leader:* Is the patient's follow-up complete? Let's make sure of that first.  
  ↳ The standard and patient safety still come first.
- *Leader:* Walk me through what happened yesterday.  
  ↳ Curiosity before judgment.
- *Team member:* The alert came in during a stretch of about 20 new alerts in an hour, and it got buried.
- *Leader:* That helps. Let's look at whether others have had the same problem, and I'll file it through our safety reporting process so we can fix how alerts show up.  
  ↳ Just culture: a human error in a system that made it easy, so fix the system and report it properly.
- **Result:** The patient is safe, the alert workflow gets fixed, and Leah and her teammates keep bringing problems to you early.

**Takeaway:** How you respond to the first reported mistake decides whether you hear about the next one. Thank them, protect the patient, get curious, fix the system.

### Common traps
- **Thinking psychological safety means lowering the bar.** Safety and high standards go together. The goal is the learning zone, not the comfort zone.
- **Asking “Any questions?” and taking silence as agreement.** Ask specific questions: “What's one thing that could go wrong with this?” Silence often means it doesn't feel safe to speak.
- **Reacting to how bad the outcome was.** Respond to the behavior. An honest slip with a bad outcome still calls for consoling and fixing the process.
- **Thanking people for bad news only when it's convenient.** Be consistent. People are watching how you react on your worst day, not your best.

### Check your understanding

    - ○ Say nothing, since the patient was fine. *Skipping it misses the chance to fix the process and to follow your safety reporting steps.*
